# Directing Workflow Reference

> Status: v0.1 operational baseline. Source-backed project materials will refine this module.

## 1. Brief to directing idea

Extract five statements before ideation:

1. **Audience now** — what they currently believe, feel or do.
2. **Audience after** — the single change the film must create.
3. **Promise** — the product or brand truth that earns that change.
4. **Proof** — what the viewer can see, hear or infer as evidence.
5. **Constraint** — mandatories, prohibitions, duration, format, market and approval limits.

Write the directing idea as: “We make the audience feel/realise **X** by letting them experience
**Y** through the visual engine **Z**.” If the idea cannot guide shot decisions, it is a theme, not a
directing idea.

For product-led narrative, also write the causal spine before ideation:

`specific obstacle → intention/trigger → product intervention → observable proof → changed state → payoff`

If the work has several features, use `commercial-dramaturgy-and-board.md` to cluster them into scenario
friction modules. Pure beauty, packshot and material films may use product-state choreography instead of
inventing a human problem.

## 2. Treatment architecture

A treatment should contain:

- One-sentence film proposition.
- Director's point of view and the reason this form fits the brief.
- Beginning, escalation, turn and resolution.
- Performance and subject behaviour.
- World, production design and material language.
- Cinematography: format, aspect ratio, lens family, camera behaviour and exposure philosophy.
- Lighting, palette, contrast, texture and finishing intent.
- Editorial rhythm, transitions, VO, sound perspective, effects and silence; no music direction.
- AIGC production method, continuity system, hero-shot risks and fallbacks.

After drafting, complete `templates/treatment-trace-matrix.md`. A treatment rule with no downstream shot
evidence is an orphan rule; a hero/proof/transition shot with no approved source is an orphan shot or proposal.

## 3. Beat sheet

Each beat needs: time range, visible event, audience information, emotional value, proof/brand job,
transition and dependency. A beat should change information, emotion, power, space or time. Merge beats
that only repeat mood.

## 4. Shot design grammar

Design in this order:

1. **Dramatic unit** — the change inside the shot.
2. **Blocking** — subject paths, eyelines, distances, entrances, exits and interaction.
3. **Viewpoint** — whose knowledge or emotional distance controls the frame.
4. **Shot size and composition** — information hierarchy, negative space and reveal strategy.
5. **Lens logic** — perspective, compression, proximity, distortion, depth and focus behaviour.
6. **Camera height/angle** — relation of viewer, subject and environment.
7. **Movement** — motivation, axis, speed, acceleration, parallax and stop point.
8. **Light and material** — direction, source logic, contrast, color separation and surface response.
9. **Time** — duration, internal rhythm, speed treatment and transition handles.
10. **Sound** — VO/dialogue, ambience, foley, action/designed SFX, sync events and silence only.
11. **Edit function** — setup, reveal, escalation, contrast, punctuation, bridge or release.

## 5. Camera and blocking patterns

Use patterns as choices, not presets:

- **Locked observation** — authority, tension, graphic precision or performance priority.
- **Motivated follow** — camera inherits a subject's trajectory; useful for discovery and momentum.
- **Counter-move** — camera and subject oppose each other; changes pressure or scale.
- **Lateral reveal** — foreground occlusion and parallax disclose information in stages.
- **Push/pull** — changes psychological distance; define the exact revelation or emotional turn.
- **Orbit** — redefines relationships or space; avoid when it only advertises production value.
- **Crane/boom transition** — changes hierarchy or connects spatial layers.
- **Handheld proximity** — embodied instability or intimacy; specify amplitude and operator behaviour.
- **POV/subjective drift** — restrict knowledge to a character or force.
- **Impossible AIGC move** — use only when the continuous transformation is the idea and continuity can hold.

## 6. Lens and depth logic

Specify a lens family or perspective intent before an exact focal length when sensor/model is unknown.

- Wide proximity expands depth and movement; protect faces and product geometry from unwanted distortion.
- Normal perspective supports natural presence and blocking clarity.
- Long-lens compression isolates layers, slows perceived travel and can create surveillance or elegance.
- Deep focus supports simultaneous information; shallow focus enforces attention but raises continuity risk.
- Macro/probe views reveal material and process; include scale cues and plausible focus travel.

## 7. Sequence continuity

Maintain a continuity bible for:

- Identity, anatomy, wardrobe, hair, makeup and performance state.
- Product geometry, logo orientation, materials and functional behaviour.
- Location topology, axis, screen direction, eyeline and travel direction.
- Time of day, key-light direction, weather, atmosphere, palette and exposure.
- Prop state, hand occupancy, cause-effect and transformation progress.
- Camera grammar, grain/texture, motion cadence and aspect ratio.

Break continuity only as an explicit transition or story device.

## 8. Production and review

Classify shots as hero, connective, proof, performance, transition or insurance. Test hero shots and
identity-critical assets early. Review in this order: brief, story, performance, continuity, camera,
look, generation defects, edit, sound, brand and delivery. Give notes as:

`Priority → shot/timecode → observed issue → consequence → intended result → proposed revision`.

Audit the shooting board in two passes: creative validity, then production/prompt readiness. A field-rich
table can still be job-poor. Use a Claim Ledger when capabilities, effects, rank, health/safety, research
status or talent facts are present.
