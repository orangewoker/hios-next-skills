## 1. Core grammar

Use these as a decision system, not a checklist that forces every device into every shot.

1. **One visual claim per shot.** A shot proves shape, material, mechanism, scale, color, or brand memory—never all six.
2. **Controlled field.** Prefer black void, white sweep, restrained monochrome, or one product-derived accent. Remove props that do not prove the promise.
3. **Progressive disclosure.** Show a clue, articulate the feature, then give the hero view. Withhold the full silhouette until it has narrative value.
4. **Mandatory rare opening.** Start from a low “architecture” angle, probe macro, interior passage, axial tunnel, impossible top view, or another scarce viewpoint. Default to giant scale. Novelty must bridge to product recognition within about 2–3 seconds.
5. **Material is the subject.** Light angle, source size, falloff, and movement must be chosen for the surface—not from a generic “cinematic lighting” preset.
6. **Precision motion.** Use clean acceleration/deceleration, stable axes, and designed endpoints. Avoid random handheld drift unless the concept intentionally contrasts precision with life.
7. **Scale contrast.** Pair macro detail with a clean hero view. A product feels monumental when the camera height, parallax, and light travel imply scale.
8. **Edit by shared property.** Match shape, axis, direction, luminance, color, scale, or sound transient.
9. **Text behaves like an object.** Let copy align, reveal, track, or settle with the shot; keep it short enough to read once.
10. **Signature ending.** Reduce motion, restore full silhouette, land promise, product name, and action in that order.

## 2. Mandatory opening grammar

The first shot must pass all four checks:

1. **Scarcity:** the audience is unlikely to see the product from this position in daily life.
2. **Scale evidence:** use at least three cues such as extreme-low camera height, partial crop, long parallax, light traveling across the surface, architectural depth, atmosphere falloff, or a truthful small scale reference.
3. **Recognition bridge:** reveal a distinctive component, label zone, silhouette, or color within 2–3 seconds.
4. **Copy evidence:** the opening line names what the viewer can see and then turns it into a supported experience or promise.

Default construction: camera at 0–5% of product height, product occupying 130–300% of frame height so the silhouette exceeds the frame, controlled 18–24 mm architectural exaggeration or a neutral 35–50 mm low-angle view, slow upward parallax, one long traveling rim light, and a designed endpoint at a recognizable feature.

Do not use distortion as a substitute for scale. Keep straight product edges credible and preserve real proportions.

## 3. Composition and camera

### Background modes

- **True void:** no floor line, no contact shadow; ideal for floating electronics and impossible assemblies.
- **Infinite sweep:** imperceptible floor-to-wall transition with a soft contact shadow; best when weight and scale matter.
- **Luminous field:** high-key white or product-color gradient; best for translucent, pale, or friendly products.
- **Split field:** two controlled colors with a clean boundary or light transition; use for variants and comparisons.

Do not automatically ban all shadows. A micro contact shadow or controlled reflection can be the evidence that prevents a product from looking pasted in.

### Lens character

- **18–24 mm equivalent:** architectural exaggeration; keep edges straight and avoid cartoon distortion.
- **35–50 mm:** neutral hero motion and assembly.
- **70–100 mm:** compressed premium portrait of the product.
- **90–120 mm macro:** texture, seams, ports, woven fibers, liquid, embossing.
- **Orthographic/telecentric look:** diagrams, top views, size comparison, precise alignment.

Use deep enough focus to prove the feature. Ultra-shallow focus is not automatically premium.

### Framing ladder

`abstract detail → recognizable feature → partial silhouette → full hero → family/variant lockup`

Skip rungs only when the total duration is short or the product is already recognizable.

## 4. Motion hierarchy

Treat camera, subject, and light as three independent channels with a shared motion budget.

- **Dominant channel:** carries the information; amplitude 100%.
- **Supporting channel:** adds parallax or material response; amplitude about 20–40%.
- **Micro channel:** nearly subliminal shimmer, breath, or alignment; amplitude under 15%.

Useful combinations:

| Information task | Camera | Subject | Light |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reveal silhouette | slow arc | near-static turn | narrow edge sweep |
| Explain mechanism | locked/short push | component opens | pulse at endpoint |
| Prove material | macro lateral slide | static | grazing sweep dominant |
| Show variants | slow pullback | products align | two pools merge |
| Final lockup | decelerating push | settles to zero | halo stabilizes |

Avoid three large motions at once. Avoid direction changes without a motivated endpoint.

## 5. Rhythm systems

### Default: reveal → articulation → signature

- **Reveal:** 35–45%; fewer cuts, longer anticipation.
- **Articulation:** 30–40%; 2–4 proof beats with match cuts.
- **Signature:** 20–30%; deceleration, full product, copy and sonic resolve.

### Alternate curves

- **Pulse:** slow → burst → pause → burst → settle. Good for 15–30 seconds and multiple features.
- **Continuous orbit:** one apparent unbroken move with hidden match cuts. Good for one-product elegance.
- **Assembly crescendo:** components enter one by one and snap together. Good for modular products.
- **Material meditation:** almost no fast section; light and sound reveal tactile details. Good for fabric, leather, glass, cosmetics.

Cut only after the viewer has recognized the intended information. Fast does not mean unreadable.

## 6. Copy and sound

- Use one thought per card, ideally 2–8 Chinese characters or one short line.
- Prefer claim architecture: `benefit → proof → name`, not a list of adjectives.
- Reveal copy after or during the visual proof; do not make text explain an unseen feature.
- Reserve clear negative space before placing copy.
- Use sound as structure: low air/rumble for reveal, precise ticks/clicks for mechanism, narrow whoosh for light travel, short tonal bloom for hero lockup.
- Avoid constant trailer bass, excessive risers, or sound effects on every micro-motion.
- Build opening copy as `visible fact → experience`, not as unsupported grandeur. Use `references/rare-opening-copy.md` for formulas and claim checks.

## 7. What to avoid

- literal Apple logos, copied slogans, exact packaging, proprietary UI, or claims of endorsement;
- generic “luxury cinematic 8K” without geometry, light direction, or action;
- liquid-metal surfaces when the real product is matte plastic or fabric;
- impossible ports, duplicated buttons, warped labels, and changing component counts;
- a new background color, lens language, and motion axis in every shot;
- illegible generated Chinese text; add final typography in post when fidelity matters;
- floating products with no visual rationale, or heavy shadows in a true-void setup;
- overuse of lens flares, dust, smoke, particles, and blue-orange grading.
- an ordinary full-frontal, eye-level, 45-degree, tabletop, or lifestyle-wide first shot;
- “岂止于…” constructions whose second half is not supported by the brief or the picture;
- copied Apple taglines, near-translations, or typography that makes the work look officially affiliated.
